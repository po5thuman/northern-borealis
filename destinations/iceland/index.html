Northern Lights in Iceland

Compare Reykjavik and Akureyri, find the best time to go, understand what actually affects your chances, and choose the right Northern Lights tour for your trip.

Compare Iceland Tour Bases

Iceland is made for a Northern Lights trip: long winter nights, huge stretches of countryside away from artificial light, and landscapes that can turn an aurora sighting into one of the defining moments of a trip. The difficult part isn't deciding whether Iceland is worth considering — it's working out where to base yourself, when to travel and whether to chase the lights independently or let a local guide handle the search.

The aurora borealis can appear across Iceland when three essentials come together: enough darkness, sufficiently clear sky and favourable solar conditions. That also means no destination, month or tour can guarantee a display. Cloud cover can make an otherwise active night useless, while a relatively modest forecast can still produce a memorable show if the sky above you is clear. If you want the science first, start with our guide to what the Northern Lights are.

For most travellers, the practical choice comes down to two bases. Reykjavik offers the widest range of Northern Lights tours in Iceland, including bus chases, small-group trips, 4x4 adventures, cruises and combination tours. Akureyri puts you in North Iceland, with smaller-scale touring and quick access to dark countryside. This page helps you choose between them without duplicating the detailed tour comparisons on those two city guides.

Which Iceland Northern Lights Base Should You Choose?

There isn't a permanent winner between Reykjavik and Akureyri. The aurora can appear over both, and the clearest patch of sky on your actual night matters more than a broad claim that one city always has “better” Northern Lights. The more useful question is which base works best for your trip.

Choose Reykjavik if...

  • this is your first trip to Iceland;
  • you want the widest choice of tour styles and prices;
  • you want to combine the aurora with the Golden Circle or other South Iceland sightseeing;
  • you prefer simple pickups and a large choice of departure dates;
  • you only have a short Iceland itinerary.

Choose Akureyri if...

  • North Iceland is already part of your itinerary;
  • you prefer a quieter, smaller-city base;
  • you want quick access to countryside with little artificial light;
  • a small-group or photography-focused experience appeals to you;
  • you've already explored Reykjavik and the south on a previous trip.

If you're staying in Reykjavik anyway, there is usually little reason to travel to Akureyri only for the aurora. Instead, give yourself several nights and let a tour operator search for favourable conditions. If North Iceland is already on your route, Akureyri becomes an excellent place to schedule your aurora night without doubling back south.

Best Places to See the Northern Lights in Iceland

The best viewing location changes from night to night because Icelandic cloud cover is highly variable. In general, you want a location that is dark, safe to reach and under a clear or partly clear section of sky. Getting away from artificial light makes faint aurora easier to see, but driving hundreds of kilometres to a famous landmark is pointless if that landmark is under cloud.

🌌 Reykjavik & Southwest Iceland

Reykjavik is Iceland's most convenient aurora-tour base. Strong displays can sometimes be seen from the city, but leaving the urban glow generally improves visibility, particularly for weaker aurora. The advantage of joining a Reykjavik tour is mobility: operators can choose viewing areas based on the night's cloud forecast rather than committing you to one fixed landmark.

🏔️ Akureyri & North Iceland

Akureyri is the practical tour base for North Iceland. The town is surrounded by mountains and open countryside around Eyjafjörður, so operators can move away from artificial light quickly. Tour choice is much smaller than in Reykjavik, but that can suit travellers who already plan to spend several days in the north.

📸 Famous Icelandic Landscapes

Þingvellir, the Snæfellsnes Peninsula, black-sand coastlines, waterfalls and other rural landscapes can create extraordinary aurora photographs when conditions line up. But don't choose a location purely because you've seen a spectacular photo from there. For an actual Northern Lights hunt, clear sky should beat famous scenery. If the clouds move, the best viewing spot can move with them.

Best Time to See the Northern Lights in Iceland

The practical Northern Lights season in Iceland begins around late August and continues into April, when the country has enough nighttime darkness for the aurora to become visible. The core travel period is September through March. There isn't one magic month that guarantees better displays: darkness determines how long you have to watch, while cloud cover and solar conditions determine what you can actually see on a particular night.

September and October: useful if you want an aurora trip without committing to the deepest part of winter. Nights are dark enough for viewing while you retain more daylight for sightseeing.

November to February: the longest nights of the season give you a large daily viewing window. The trade-off is winter weather, limited daylight and potentially more challenging road conditions.

March into early April: nights remain dark enough for aurora viewing while daylight returns quickly. March is particularly attractive if you want to combine daytime exploration with evening aurora hunting.

Late spring and summer: not suitable for a conventional Northern Lights trip. Auroral activity doesn't simply switch off, but Iceland's bright summer sky prevents you from seeing it. July is therefore the wrong month if the Northern Lights are a major reason for your visit.

For a broader month-by-month comparison, including the trade-offs between darkness, weather and daylight, read our complete guide to the best time to see the Northern Lights.

How to Improve Your Chances of Seeing the Northern Lights in Iceland

No honest guide can promise you the aurora, but you can make better decisions. These are the factors that matter most when you're actually in Iceland:

Give yourself more than one night. A three- or four-night stay gives Icelandic weather more chances to cooperate than a single fixed evening. If the Northern Lights are a priority, don't leave your only attempt until the last night.

Check cloud cover, not just aurora activity. The Icelandic Meteorological Office combines cloud information with its aurora forecast for a reason: you need an opening in the cloud to see what's happening above it. A dramatic aurora forecast is no use from beneath solid overcast.

Don't obsess over the Kp number. Kp is useful context, but it isn't a brightness guarantee and shouldn't be your only decision tool in Iceland. Clear sky and real-time space-weather conditions matter too. Our Northern Lights forecast page gives you a starting point, while our how-to guide explains how to interpret the different signals.

Get away from unnecessary artificial light. Bright surroundings reduce contrast and make weak displays harder to recognise. You don't always need to travel far — you need a safe location with a dark enough foreground and a useful opening in the clouds.

Be patient. Aurora intensity can change quickly. A quiet-looking sky at the start of the evening doesn't tell you what it will look like an hour later.

Northern Lights Tour or See Them on Your Own?

You do not need a tour to see the Northern Lights in Iceland. If you have a rental car, suitable winter-driving experience, safe road conditions and confidence using Icelandic weather forecasts, an independent aurora hunt gives you complete freedom. But a tour solves several practical problems at once.

A tour makes the logistics easier. Someone else handles the night driving, watches the weather and chooses where to stop. That's particularly valuable in winter, when wind, ice and changing road conditions can turn a simple-looking drive into something much less comfortable.

A tour can move with the weather. Good aurora hunting isn't about driving to “the Northern Lights spot.” It's about finding a sufficiently clear patch of sky within a sensible driving radius. Local operators do that repeatedly throughout the season.

Different tours suit different travellers. Reykjavik has everything from larger coach tours and small-group 4x4 trips to cruises and sightseeing combinations. Akureyri has a smaller selection, including more intimate and photography-oriented options. That's why the individual city pages contain the actual tour rankings and booking links rather than repeating them here.

Frequently Asked Questions

📍 What is the best place to see the Northern Lights in Iceland?
There isn't one location that is best every night because cloud cover changes quickly across Iceland. Dark places away from artificial light are generally better. Reykjavik is the most convenient base for tour choice and departures, while Akureyri gives you easy access to quieter countryside in North Iceland. On the night itself, clear sky matters more than chasing a famous location.
🌌 When can you see the Northern Lights in Iceland?
The practical viewing season runs from late August into April, when Iceland has enough nighttime darkness. September through March is the core period for most aurora trips. The Northern Lights can occur outside those months too, but Iceland's bright summer sky prevents normal viewing.
🗓️ What is the best time to see the Northern Lights in Iceland?
September through March gives travellers long enough nights for aurora viewing. Midwinter provides the longest daily viewing window, while September and March can also be excellent. There is no guaranteed best month: darkness, cloud cover and solar conditions all have to cooperate. See our full best-time guide for the month-by-month trade-offs.
🏙️ Can you see the Northern Lights from Reykjavik?
Yes. Strong aurora can be visible from Reykjavik when skies are clear, but artificial light makes weaker displays harder to see. That's why most Reykjavik Northern Lights tours drive away from the city and choose viewing locations according to the night's conditions.
⚖️ Is Reykjavik or Akureyri better for Northern Lights tours?
They suit different itineraries. Reykjavik offers far more tour choice and easier logistics for most first-time visitors. Akureyri works particularly well if you're already exploring North Iceland and prefer a quieter base with quick access to dark countryside. Weather on your actual dates can matter more than the city itself.
🚐 Do I need a Northern Lights tour in Iceland?
No. You can hunt independently if you have suitable transport, safe driving conditions and know how to use weather and aurora forecasts. A guided tour is convenient because the operator handles the driving, watches changing conditions and chooses the viewing area. Read our complete how-to guide before deciding.
☀️ Can you see the Northern Lights in Iceland in July?
Not under normal viewing conditions. Auroral activity can still occur, but Iceland's summer sky remains too bright in July for the Northern Lights to be visible. If seeing the aurora is a major goal, plan your trip during the darker part of the year instead.
