How to Read the Northern Lights Forecast

The forecast at the top of this page displays the maximum predicted Kp index for each of the next three days. The Kp index is a global scale, running from 0 to 9, that measures geomagnetic activity in Earth's atmosphere. In simple terms: the higher the number, the stronger the aurora, and the further south from the North Pole it becomes visible.

The two map images show NOAA's "view line" — the further south that red band pushes on the map, the more people across the globe will be able to see the lights. If you are standing north of that line on a clear night, your chances are excellent.

What Is a Good Kp Index for the Aurora?

This is the question every aurora hunter asks. The honest answer is: it depends entirely on your latitude. The closer you are to the Arctic Circle, the lower the Kp number you need.

In the Arctic (Kp 2–3 is enough): If you are in a prime destination inside the auroral oval — like Tromsø, Abisko, or Finnish Lapland — the aurora is often directly overhead. Here, even a quiet Kp of 2 or 3 can produce a spectacular display. This is exactly why these are the best places to see the northern lights.

Mid-latitudes (Kp 5+ needed): If you are further south, you need a geomagnetic storm (Kp 5 or higher, classified as a "G1" storm or above) to push the aurora down to your location. These stronger events are less frequent but do happen, especially around the equinoxes.

⚡ The Golden Rule of Aurora Hunting: A clear sky always beats a high Kp index. You could have an incredible Kp 7 storm, but if it's cloudy, you'll see nothing. Always check your local weather forecast for clear skies alongside this aurora forecast — the two must line up. This is the single biggest tip in our guide on how to see the northern lights.

What Causes the Aurora Borealis Forecast to Change?

The northern lights are created when charged particles from the sun — carried by the solar wind — collide with gases in Earth's upper atmosphere. When the sun is active, it releases huge bursts of these particles, sometimes in the form of solar flares or coronal mass ejections (CMEs).

NOAA's satellites monitor this solar activity around the clock. When a burst of solar wind is detected heading towards Earth, the forecast Kp index rises. It typically takes 2–3 days for these particles to travel the 93 million miles to Earth, which is exactly why a reliable forecast can only be given three days in advance. If you'd like a deeper dive into the physics, our guide on what the northern lights are explains the whole process.

Combining the Forecast with the Best Time to Travel

While this forecast tells you about activity over the next 72 hours, seeing the aurora also depends on the season. The lights are only visible when the sky is dark enough, which rules out the summer months in the Arctic due to the midnight sun.

The season generally runs from late August to early April. For a complete month-by-month breakdown of when the skies are darkest and the activity is highest, see our full guide on the best time to see the northern lights.
