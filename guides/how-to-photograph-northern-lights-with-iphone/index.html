How to Photograph the Northern Lights With an iPhone

You can capture the aurora without carrying a dedicated camera. Here's how to use iPhone Night mode, stability, exposure controls and the right lens to give yourself the best chance of a sharp Northern Lights photo.

Yes — an iPhone can photograph the Northern Lights. You do not need to understand DSLR settings or manually choose an ISO and shutter speed to get started. On supported iPhones, Night mode handles much of the low-light exposure automatically. Your biggest jobs are to keep the phone completely stable, choose a suitable camera, give Night mode enough time to work and avoid overexposing a bright aurora.

This guide focuses specifically on the native iPhone Camera app. If you are using a DSLR or mirrorless camera and want manual shutter speed, aperture, ISO and focusing advice, see our complete Northern Lights photography guide.

The exact controls available depend on your iPhone model and iOS version. Night mode is available on iPhone 11 and later, while features such as Apple ProRAW are limited to supported models. That is why the method below focuses first on controls that ordinary iPhone users can actually access.

Quick iPhone Settings for Northern Lights

If the aurora has appeared and you do not have time to read the whole guide, start here.

Fast setup: rear camera · start at 1x · Photo mode · Night mode on · phone completely stable · Night mode capture time toward Max · flash off · Live Photos off · avoid digital zoom.

If the aurora is very bright and moving quickly, compare the longest Night mode shot with a shorter one. A longer capture can brighten a faint display, but it can also smooth out the fine structure of a rapidly moving aurora.

Camera Start with the rear 1x Main camera.
Night mode Use it when available and adjust the capture duration.
Stability Tripod or completely secure surface.
Flash Off. It cannot illuminate the aurora.
Live Photos Keep off while using Night mode.
Zoom Avoid pinching into digital zoom in darkness.
If you remember only one thing: stabilise the iPhone. Night mode can use a multi-second capture, and movement during that process is one of the easiest ways to lose detail.

How to Take Northern Lights Photos on iPhone: Step by Step

Open the native Camera app in Photo mode Start with the standard Camera app rather than complicating your first attempt with manual third-party controls.
Select the rear 1x camera The Main camera is the safest starting point for low-light photography. You can experiment with other cameras once you have secured a good shot.
Make the iPhone completely stable Mount it on a tripod or brace it securely against a solid object. Do not try to hold a long Night mode capture perfectly still in your hands if you have a stable alternative.
Let Night mode activate When the iPhone detects sufficiently low light, Night mode appears automatically on supported models.
Choose a longer Night mode capture Open the Night mode control and move the duration toward Max. The maximum offered depends on the darkness and how stable the iPhone detects the device to be.
Compose with some landscape in the frame Mountains, trees, cabins, snow, water or a person can give the aurora scale and make the photograph more interesting than an empty green sky.
Tap a distant part of the scene if focus needs help Choose a distant, contrasty area rather than a nearby foreground object if the sky is the priority. You can touch and hold the focus area to engage AE/AF Lock when useful.
Press the shutter and leave the phone alone Do not move the phone while the Night mode timer is counting down. If alignment crosshairs appear, keep them aligned.
Check the result and shoot again Zoom into the photograph to check detail. Then try a shorter or longer Night mode capture as the aurora changes rather than relying on a single shot.

How to Use iPhone Night Mode for the Northern Lights

Night mode is the most important native iPhone feature for aurora photography. On supported iPhones it activates automatically when the camera detects a dark environment. A number beside the Night mode icon shows how long the capture will take.

To adjust it, open the camera controls, select Night mode and move the capture-time control toward Max. Your iPhone decides what maximum duration is available based on the scene and the stability of the device.

This is an important distinction from a traditional camera. On most iPhones you are not manually setting a conventional 10-, 20- or 30-second shutter speed in Apple's standard Camera app. You are selecting the Night mode capture duration available to you, while the iPhone combines exposure and computational processing behind the scenes.

If the iPhone detects movement during the capture, alignment crosshairs can appear on screen. Keep them aligned until the exposure finishes.

Do not assume “Max” is always the best-looking result. Max is excellent for gathering light from a faint aurora, but a strong, rapidly changing display may retain more visible structure in a shorter capture. Take both and compare.

Why can't I see Night mode?

First make sure your iPhone supports it. Night mode is available on iPhone 11 and later, but its availability on individual cameras and other camera modes varies by model. You also need sufficiently low light for the feature to engage.

Keep flash and Live Photos off while you are trying to use Night mode. Enabling either can deactivate Night mode.

Do You Need a Tripod for iPhone Northern Lights Photography?

You can sometimes capture the aurora handheld, particularly when it is bright, but a tripod is one of the biggest improvements you can make. The phone can recognise when it is more stable and Night mode can offer longer capture options in suitable conditions.

You do not need a large photography tripod. A compact phone tripod with a secure mount is enough, provided it remains stable in wind and on snow or ice.

If you do not have one, use a wall, bench, car roof, rock or another genuinely stable surface. Prop the phone securely, compose the image and avoid touching it during the exposure. Be particularly careful on a car: people moving inside, closing doors or an idling engine can introduce vibration.

Cold-weather tip: keep the iPhone protected and reasonably warm between shooting sessions. Cold can reduce battery performance, so start the night with plenty of charge and consider carrying a power bank if you will be outside for several hours.

Should You Use 0.5x, 1x or Zoom for Northern Lights?

Start with 1x. The rear Main camera is generally the most dependable choice when light levels are extremely low. It is the camera we would use first to secure the photograph before experimenting.

The 0.5x Ultra Wide camera can be fantastic when the aurora stretches across a huge area of sky or when you want a dramatic foreground. However, camera specifications and Night mode support differ across iPhone generations, and an Ultra Wide camera may not perform as well as the Main camera in very dark conditions.

Avoid using digital zoom simply to make the aurora look larger. In darkness, cropping into the image digitally can exaggerate noise and reduce detail. If you want a tighter composition, try the available optical camera choices on your model or crop the final photograph carefully afterward.

Choice When to use it Our approach
1x Main General aurora photography Start here
0.5x Ultra Wide Huge sky, dramatic landscape Experiment after securing the 1x shot
Telephoto Tighter landscape details on capable models Use cautiously in very low light
Digital zoom Rarely necessary for aurora Avoid where possible

How to Focus and Adjust Exposure on iPhone

The iPhone normally handles focus and exposure automatically, and that is often enough. If it is struggling, tap a distant, contrasty part of the landscape or sky area you want prioritised.

To lock the selected focus and exposure area, touch and hold it until AE/AF Lock appears. This can stop the camera from continually changing its decision while you recompose.

You can also adjust the image brightness using the exposure control. This is useful when a very bright aurora is turning into a pale, over-bright mass. Reducing the exposure slightly can preserve more colour and visible structure.

Bright aurora? Do not assume the photograph needs to be as bright as possible. A slightly darker exposure can preserve the shape and colour of a strong display and can make the night scene look more natural.

Should You Use Apple ProRAW for Northern Lights?

If your iPhone supports Apple ProRAW and you plan to edit your photographs, it is worth experimenting with. ProRAW retains more image information and gives you greater flexibility when adjusting exposure, colour and white balance than a standard processed image.

On supported models, enable it under Settings → Camera → Formats, then switch RAW on in the Camera app when you want to use it.

There are two important catches. First, ProRAW files are substantially larger than ordinary HEIF or JPEG photographs. Second, available resolutions depend on the model and shooting mode. For example, switching to Night mode can reduce the resolution options available on some Pro iPhones, so do not assume that a displayed maximum-resolution specification applies unchanged to every low-light capture.

ProRAW is therefore an optional tool, not a requirement. If you simply want a good aurora photograph to share, standard Night mode processing can produce a very usable result without creating huge files.

How to Compose Better iPhone Northern Lights Photos

A common first attempt is to fill almost the entire photograph with sky. It proves you saw the aurora, but it often does not communicate where you were or how the experience felt.

Try including a recognisable foreground: a snowy mountain, cabin, road, tree line, fjord, frozen lake or another person. Reflections can work particularly well when you have open water. A foreground also gives the photograph depth and makes the scale of the aurora easier to understand.

Keep an eye on the horizon too. In darkness it is easy to concentrate entirely on the green sky and discover later that the horizon is noticeably tilted.

If the aurora is directly overhead, try a vertical composition as well as a horizontal one. If it forms a low arc above distant mountains, a horizontal frame may suit the scene better. Shoot both while the display gives you the opportunity.

How to Edit Northern Lights Photos on iPhone

You do not need to make the aurora fluorescent green for the photograph to work. Start with small adjustments and aim to preserve detail in both the sky and landscape.

In the Photos app, useful adjustments can include Exposure, Brilliance, Highlights, Shadows, Contrast, Black Point, Warmth and Noise Reduction. The exact amount depends entirely on the photograph, so there is no sensible universal slider recipe.

If the aurora is losing detail, try reducing Highlights before making the entire image darker. If the foreground is almost black, gently lift Shadows. If the sky has become grey from excessive brightening, restore some Black Point or Contrast.

Be conservative with saturation. Cameras can already record aurora colour more strongly than it appears to the naked eye during faint displays. Heavy saturation can quickly produce an unnatural result.

What about ProRAW editing?

ProRAW gives you more room to adjust exposure, colour and white balance, which can be particularly useful when the aurora and foreground differ greatly in brightness. You can edit ProRAW directly in Apple's Photos app or use compatible editing software if you want more control.

Why Do My iPhone Northern Lights Photos Look Bad?

The aurora is blurry

The phone may have moved during the Night mode capture, or the aurora itself may have moved substantially during a long capture. Stabilise the phone first. If the aurora is bright and fast, compare the result with a shorter Night mode duration.

The photo is very grainy

The scene may simply be extremely dark. Stability helps the iPhone collect more useful information without relying entirely on aggressive brightening. Try the 1x Main camera, avoid digital zoom and look for a composition where the aurora is clearly visible rather than forcing an almost invisible display into a bright photograph.

The aurora looks white or washed out

A bright display can be overexposed. Reduce the exposure slightly or try a shorter Night mode capture and compare the colour and structure.

Night mode only gives me a short capture

The available duration is determined by the conditions and device stability. Mounting the iPhone securely can allow the camera to offer a longer capture when appropriate.

Night mode disappeared

Check whether flash or Live Photos has been enabled, as these can deactivate Night mode. Also remember that Night mode only appears in sufficiently low light and support differs by camera and iPhone model.

The 0.5x photo looks worse than 1x

That can happen in very low light. Use the 1x Main camera for your safe shot, then treat the Ultra Wide as an alternative composition rather than assuming wider is automatically better.

iPhone vs Camera for Northern Lights Photography

An iPhone is excellent when you want something small, fast and easy to carry. A dedicated camera still gives you substantially more direct control over shutter speed, aperture, ISO, focusing and lens choice, which matters when you want to freeze fast aurora movement or optimise image quality in difficult conditions.

If photography is one of the main reasons for your trip, read our full Northern Lights camera settings guide. If your priority is enjoying the trip and bringing home good photographs without carrying a camera bag, an iPhone can be more than enough when the conditions cooperate.

The Best iPhone Setting Cannot Fix Cloud Cover

Phone technique only matters once you can actually see the sky. Cloud cover, darkness and your location are fundamental, so give yourself several possible viewing nights rather than relying on one evening.

iPhone Northern Lights Photography FAQs

📱 Can you photograph the Northern Lights with an iPhone?
Yes. iPhones with Night mode can photograph the Northern Lights, particularly when the aurora is visible and the phone is kept very stable. Night mode is available on iPhone 11 and later, although the cameras and low-light capabilities vary between models.
⚙️ What iPhone settings should I use for Northern Lights?
Start with the rear 1x Main camera in Photo mode, keep the phone completely stable, allow Night mode to activate and move its available capture duration toward Max. Keep flash and Live Photos off and avoid digital zoom. If a bright, fast aurora becomes too smooth or washed out, compare the result with a shorter Night mode capture.
🌙 How do I get a longer Night mode exposure on iPhone?
Open the Night mode control and move the capture-time slider toward Max. The maximum offered depends on the scene and how stable the iPhone is. A tripod or secure surface can help the phone offer longer Night mode captures when conditions allow.
🔭 Should I use 1x or 0.5x for Northern Lights?
Start with the 1x Main camera because it is generally the safest choice in very low light. The 0.5x Ultra Wide camera can create a dramatic view of a large aurora, but low-light performance and Night mode support vary between iPhone generations.
🗂️ Should I use Apple ProRAW for Northern Lights?
If your iPhone supports ProRAW and you plan to edit the photograph, it can provide more flexibility for exposure, colour and white balance adjustments. It is not essential, however, and ProRAW files use considerably more storage.
📐 Do I need a tripod for Northern Lights photos on iPhone?
Not always, but it is strongly recommended. Night mode can involve multi-second captures, so keeping the phone stable improves consistency. If you do not have a tripod, place the iPhone securely on or against a stationary object and avoid touching it until the capture finishes.
