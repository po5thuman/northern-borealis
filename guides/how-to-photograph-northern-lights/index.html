How to Photograph the Northern Lights

Camera settings, shutter speed, ISO, aperture and focusing explained — plus how to adapt when the aurora suddenly gets brighter or starts moving faster.

Photographing the Northern Lights is less complicated than it first appears. The challenge is not finding one perfect camera setting — it is learning how to change your exposure as the aurora changes. A faint green arc may need several seconds of exposure, while a bright, fast-moving curtain can lose its shape if you leave the shutter open for too long.

This guide explains the complete Northern Lights photography workflow for a DSLR or mirrorless camera: what equipment you actually need, the best camera settings to start with, how to choose shutter speed, ISO and aperture, how to focus in the dark, and what to change when your photographs are too dark, too bright, blurry or noisy.

If you're still planning when and where to photograph the aurora, start with our guides to the best time to see the Northern Lights and the best places to see the Northern Lights. If you're already outside waiting for a display, check the Northern Lights forecast alongside local cloud conditions.

Quick Northern Lights Camera Settings

If the aurora appears and you need a starting point immediately, put your camera on a tripod and begin with the settings below. They are a starting exposure, not a rule.

Starting setup: Manual exposure · f/2.8 · ISO 1600 · 5–10 seconds · manual focus · RAW · white balance around 3500–4000 K.

Take a test photograph and check both the aurora and stars. If the lights are moving quickly, shorten the shutter speed. If the image then becomes too dark, raise the ISO before sacrificing the movement and structure you want to preserve.

Aurora Shutter speed Aperture ISO
Bright & fast-moving About 1–4 sec f/1.4–f/2.8 800–3200
Moderate About 5–10 sec f/1.8–f/2.8 800–3200
Faint & slow About 10–20 sec f/1.4–f/2.8 1600–3200+

The ranges overlap deliberately. Aurora brightness, moonlight, snow, light pollution, lens speed and your camera's sensor all affect the exposure. A setting that works beautifully one minute may need changing a few minutes later.

The most important rule: choose your shutter speed according to the movement of the aurora, then use aperture and ISO to obtain the exposure you need. Fast aurora rewards a faster shutter.

What Equipment Do You Need for Northern Lights Photography?

You do not need the most expensive camera available. The most useful combination is a camera that allows manual control, a reasonably fast wide-angle lens and a stable tripod.

A DSLR or mirrorless camera with manual exposure. You need control over shutter speed, aperture and ISO. Modern APS-C and Micro Four Thirds cameras can produce excellent aurora photographs too; full frame is useful in low light, but it is not a requirement.

A wide, fast lens. A wide-angle lens makes it easier to include both the aurora and the landscape, while a large maximum aperture lets more light reach the sensor. An f/2.8 lens is an excellent starting point, while f/1.8 or f/1.4 can give you more flexibility when the aurora is faint or moving quickly. If your lens only opens to f/3.5 or f/4, you can still photograph the aurora — you may simply need a higher ISO or longer exposure.

A sturdy tripod. Exposures lasting several seconds cannot normally be handheld sharply. Extend the thickest leg sections first, keep the centre column low where possible, and make sure the tripod is stable before taking the shot.

A way to avoid touching the camera during exposure. A remote release is useful, but it is not essential. A two-second self-timer or exposure-delay feature can also prevent movement caused by pressing the shutter button.

Spare power. Cold conditions can reduce usable battery performance. Keep a spare battery somewhere warmer, such as an inside pocket, rather than leaving every battery exposed to the cold.

Best Camera Settings for Northern Lights Photography

Before the aurora becomes active, set up the camera so you are not searching through menus in darkness. The following configuration works as a reliable baseline for most dedicated cameras.

Exposure modeManual (M)
File formatRAW, or RAW + JPEG
FocusManual after focusing
ApertureUsually as wide as practical
ISOStart around 1600
ShutterStart around 5–10 sec

If your camera has long-exposure noise reduction enabled, consider whether you actually want it during an active display. On many cameras the feature takes a second dark exposure after the photograph, during which you may be unable to take another shot. That can mean missing a rapidly changing aurora. High-ISO noise reduction and RAW processing behaviour vary by camera, so check your camera manual rather than assuming every model handles these settings identically.

Image stabilisation also depends on the camera and lens. With a firmly mounted camera, many photographers disable lens or sensor stabilisation, but modern systems differ. Follow the manufacturer's recommendation for your specific body and lens if you are unsure.

What Shutter Speed Should You Use for the Northern Lights?

Shutter speed is the setting that most strongly changes the appearance of the aurora. The Northern Lights move. During a long exposure, the camera records that movement continuously, so fine curtains and rays can merge into a softer green shape.

For a bright, energetic display, try approximately 1–4 seconds. This can preserve folds, rays and sharp structure. For moderate movement, roughly 5–10 seconds is a useful working range. If the aurora is faint and moving slowly, 10–20 seconds may collect enough light to reveal colour and structure that is difficult to see clearly with your eyes.

Do not automatically use 20 or 30 seconds just because it is dark. A long exposure can make a weak aurora look stronger, but it can also turn an active display into a smooth green blur. Very long exposures can also begin to show movement in the stars as the Earth rotates.

If the aurora suddenly explodes overhead: shorten the shutter speed first. If that makes the exposure too dark, raise the ISO. Preserving the structure of a fast-moving display is usually more useful than forcing the ISO to remain low.

What Is the Best Aperture for Northern Lights Photography?

In most situations, use a wide aperture. If your lens opens to f/2.8, f/2, f/1.8 or f/1.4, the extra light allows you to use a shorter shutter speed or lower ISO.

However, the widest possible aperture is not automatically the sharpest setting for every lens. Some lenses show softer corners, coma or other aberrations when used completely wide open. If the aurora is bright enough, stopping an f/1.4 lens down slightly may improve image quality while still gathering plenty of light.

Depth of field matters too. If you place a person, tree, cabin or other foreground object very close to the camera, focusing on the stars at a wide aperture may leave that foreground soft. In that situation you can either accept the shallow depth of field, focus differently for the composition, or make separate sky and foreground exposures to combine later.

What ISO Should You Use for the Northern Lights?

ISO 1600 is a useful starting point, not a target you must keep. Bright aurora, moonlight or reflective snow may allow ISO 800 or lower. A dim display photographed with a short exposure may require ISO 3200 or beyond.

Higher ISO generally brings more visible noise and reduces some editing latitude, but using an ISO that is too low can force you into a shutter speed that smears the aurora. It can also leave the image badly underexposed. In practice, a properly exposed photograph at a higher ISO can be more useful than an excessively dark frame chosen only to keep the ISO number low.

The usable ISO range varies significantly between cameras, so learn what ISO 1600, 3200 and higher actually look like on your own camera before the trip if possible.

How to Focus for Northern Lights Photography

Focus is one of the easiest ways to ruin an otherwise excellent aurora photograph. Autofocus can struggle against a dark sky, and simply turning the focus ring until it reaches the infinity symbol is not always accurate enough.

A reliable method is to switch to manual focus, point the camera at a bright star or very distant light, and magnify the live-view image as much as your camera allows. Slowly adjust focus until the star becomes the smallest, sharpest point you can achieve. Take a test photograph and zoom into the stars during playback to confirm they are sharp.

Once focus is correct, leave it alone. Be careful when changing composition, filters or gloves around the lens because it is surprisingly easy to knock the focus ring in darkness.

If you can visit your shooting location during daylight, another option is to focus accurately on a suitably distant object beforehand, switch the lens to manual focus and verify the result again after dark.

White Balance and RAW: Getting Natural Aurora Colours

For a consistent starting point, try a manual white balance around 3500–4000 K. This often keeps snowy landscapes and night skies from becoming excessively warm while retaining the colour of the aurora.

There is no universal Kelvin value for every scene. Moonlight, artificial lighting, snow and your own creative preference all affect the result. This is one reason to photograph in RAW: white balance can be adjusted far more freely during editing without committing to the camera's JPEG rendering.

RAW files also give you more flexibility when recovering shadows, controlling highlights, reducing noise and adjusting colour. If you want photographs ready to share immediately, RAW + JPEG is a useful compromise if you have enough memory-card space.

Watch the green channel

A strong aurora can become overexposed even when the overall photograph does not look obviously too bright on the rear screen. If your camera offers an RGB histogram, check whether the green channel is clipping against the right edge. If it is, shorten the exposure, lower the ISO or close the aperture slightly.

How to Compose Better Northern Lights Photos

The aurora may be the reason you pressed the shutter, but the landscape is often what turns the photograph into a memorable image. Instead of pointing the camera straight at an empty patch of green sky, look for something that gives the scene scale and location.

Mountains, fjords, frozen lakes, cabins, trees, roads and people can all provide a strong foreground. Reflections are particularly effective when you are photographing beside unfrozen water. A person beneath the aurora can also communicate the enormous scale of the display, although a person who moves during a multi-second exposure will blur.

Do not assume the widest focal length is always the most interesting. An ultra-wide lens is useful when the aurora fills the sky, but a slightly longer focal length can isolate individual curtains, mountains or reflections and create a completely different photograph.

What if the foreground is too dark?

A good exposure for a bright aurora can leave an unlit foreground almost black. If the scene is static and you are comfortable editing, you can make one exposure for the aurora and another, longer exposure for the landscape. The important point is to keep the result believable and to avoid presenting a composite as a single exposure if that distinction matters in the context where you publish it.

How to Take Pictures of the Northern Lights: Step by Step

1. Set up before the display becomes intense. Put the camera on the tripod, compose the scene and establish focus while you have time.

2. Switch to manual exposure. Start around f/2.8, ISO 1600 and 5–10 seconds.

3. Shoot RAW. Set a manual white balance around 3500–4000 K if you want consistent previews.

4. Take a test photograph. Zoom into the stars to check focus and inspect the aurora for blown highlights.

5. Watch the movement. If the aurora becomes brighter or faster, shorten the shutter speed. If it becomes faint, consider a longer exposure or higher ISO.

6. Keep shooting as the display changes. Aurora photography is dynamic. The best settings at 10:00 p.m. may be wrong at 10:02 p.m.

7. Change compositions. Once you have a safe shot, experiment with foregrounds, vertical frames, reflections and tighter focal lengths.

Northern Lights Photography Troubleshooting

My aurora looks like a blurry green cloud

Your shutter speed may be too long for the speed of the display. Try reducing a 10-second exposure to 5 seconds, then 2 seconds if the aurora is particularly active. Raise ISO as needed to compensate.

My photo is too dark

First decide whether you can afford a longer shutter speed without losing aurora structure. If not, open the aperture further or increase ISO. Do not automatically lengthen the exposure if the aurora is moving quickly.

My aurora is washed out or has lost detail

The aurora may be overexposed. Shorten the shutter speed or lower ISO and check the histogram, particularly the green channel if your camera displays individual RGB histograms.

The stars are blurry

Zoom into a star and check manual focus again. If focus is correct but the stars are elongated, the shutter may be long enough for the Earth's rotation to become visible, or the tripod may have moved during the exposure.

My foreground is sharp but the stars are not

You may be focused too close. For a scene with a very near foreground and stars at infinity, one exposure may not keep everything critically sharp at a wide aperture. Recompose, adjust the focus strategy, or consider separate focused exposures when appropriate.

There is too much noise

If the aurora is bright enough, reduce ISO. But do not lower ISO simply to chase a cleaner file if doing so forces an excessively long exposure. Modern noise reduction can help; lost aurora structure cannot be recreated as easily.

How to Photograph the Northern Lights With an iPhone or Smartphone

You do not necessarily need a dedicated camera. Recent smartphones can capture surprisingly good aurora photographs, especially when the Northern Lights are bright and the phone is held completely still.

The workflow is different, however. Night modes, exposure controls, computational processing and available RAW options vary by phone model, so DSLR and mirrorless settings should not simply be copied across to a smartphone.

We've therefore separated phone photography into its own guide. See how to photograph the Northern Lights with an iPhone for the phone-specific setup, stability techniques and settings.

Give Yourself More Than One Chance to Photograph the Aurora

Camera technique cannot fix cloud cover. If Northern Lights photography is an important part of your trip, build several possible viewing nights into your itinerary and pay attention to both aurora activity and local weather. Northern Lights Photography FAQs

📷 What are the best camera settings for the Northern Lights?
A useful starting point is manual exposure, f/2.8, ISO 1600 and roughly 5–10 seconds. From there, adjust for the actual aurora. Bright, fast-moving displays usually benefit from a shorter shutter speed, while faint aurora may need a longer exposure or higher ISO.
⏱️ What shutter speed should I use for the Northern Lights?
Try around 1–4 seconds for bright, fast-moving aurora, roughly 5–10 seconds for moderate activity and around 10–20 seconds for faint or slow aurora. These are working ranges rather than fixed rules. The faster the aurora moves, the more important a shorter exposure becomes if you want to preserve its structure.
🎚️ What ISO should I use for Northern Lights photography?
ISO 1600 is a useful starting point on many modern cameras. Depending on the scene you may be able to use ISO 800 or need ISO 3200 or higher. Use the ISO required to support the shutter speed and aperture you need rather than treating one value as universally best.
🔭 How do I focus a camera for the Northern Lights?
Switch to manual focus and use magnified live view on a bright star or very distant light. Adjust until the star appears as small and sharp as possible, then take a test shot and check it at high magnification. Do not rely blindly on the infinity mark on the lens.
🗂️ Should I photograph the Northern Lights in RAW or JPEG?
RAW is normally preferable because it preserves more information and gives you much greater control over white balance, shadows, highlights, colour and noise during editing. RAW + JPEG is useful if you also want an immediately shareable version.
📱 Can I photograph the Northern Lights with an iPhone?
Yes. Recent iPhones can photograph the aurora, particularly when the phone is kept stable and Night mode is used appropriately. See our dedicated iPhone Northern Lights photography guide for the phone-specific workflow.
