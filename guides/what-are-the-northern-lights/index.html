What Are the Northern Lights?

What causes the aurora borealis, how does it form, why does it move and change colour, and what is actually happening above Earth? Here is the science explained clearly.

Explore the Science

The Northern Lights — scientifically known as the aurora borealis — are a natural light display created when energetic charged particles collide with gases in Earth's upper atmosphere. Most visible aurora is produced by electrons accelerated within Earth's magnetosphere and guided along magnetic field lines toward the polar regions, where collisions with oxygen and nitrogen release energy as light.

The result can be a faint arc on the northern horizon or an enormous display of glowing curtains, rays and ribbons moving across the sky. Green is the colour most often associated with the aurora, but strong displays can also contain red, blue, pink, purple and white.

The phenomenon begins with the Sun but depends on Earth's magnetic environment and atmosphere. In this guide, we'll explain what causes the Northern Lights, how the aurora borealis forms, how it works, why the lights appear to dance, what creates the different colours, and when and where they can be seen.

What Are the Northern Lights?

The Northern Lights are the visible result of energetic particles interacting with gases high above Earth's surface. In the Northern Hemisphere, the phenomenon is called the aurora borealis. The corresponding display around the Southern Hemisphere's polar region is the aurora australis, or Southern Lights.

Auroras typically form in the upper atmosphere, roughly 80 to 500 kilometres above Earth. The light itself is produced when energetic particles — predominantly electrons — collide with atmospheric oxygen and nitrogen. Those collisions transfer energy to the atoms and molecules. When they release that extra energy, they emit photons at characteristic wavelengths, producing the colours we see from the ground.

The Northern Lights are therefore not a reflection, a cloud or a conventional weather event. They are a visible expression of the connection between the Sun, Earth's magnetosphere and the upper atmosphere.

What Causes the Northern Lights and How Are They Formed?

The Northern Lights form through a chain of events connecting the Sun, Earth's magnetosphere and the upper atmosphere. A common simplified explanation says that particles from the Sun simply hit Earth's atmosphere. The real process is more interesting: the solar wind transfers energy into Earth's magnetic environment, and that energy helps accelerate electrons already within the magnetosphere toward the atmosphere.

1

The Sun sends out the solar wind

The Sun continuously releases a stream of electrically charged particles and magnetic field into space called the solar wind. Conditions in that solar wind vary. Coronal mass ejections and high-speed streams from coronal holes can disturb Earth's magnetosphere and produce stronger geomagnetic activity.

2

The solar wind interacts with Earth's magnetosphere

Earth's magnetic field creates a region of space called the magnetosphere. Most solar-wind particles are diverted around it, but the solar wind can transfer energy into this magnetic system — particularly when the magnetic field carried by the solar wind is favourably oriented relative to Earth's field.

3

Electrons are accelerated toward the polar atmosphere

Energy stored in the magnetosphere can accelerate electrons along Earth's magnetic field lines toward the upper atmosphere. Because those field lines converge toward the magnetic polar regions, auroral activity tends to form ring-shaped zones around the poles known as the auroral ovals.

4

The electrons collide with oxygen and nitrogen

As the energetic electrons enter the upper atmosphere, they collide with oxygen atoms and nitrogen atoms and molecules. These collisions transfer energy to the atmospheric gases and leave them temporarily in excited energy states.

5

The atmosphere releases that energy as light

Excited atoms and molecules do not remain in those higher-energy states indefinitely. As they return to lower-energy states, they release photons. The wavelength — and therefore the colour — depends on the gas, its energy state and the altitude at which the emission occurs. Vast numbers of these emissions together create the Northern Lights.

How Do the Northern Lights Work?

The key to understanding how the Northern Lights work is to think of the aurora as the final visible stage of a much larger Sun–Earth energy system. The Sun supplies energy through the solar wind. Earth's magnetosphere receives and stores some of that energy. Electromagnetic processes then accelerate electrons toward the polar upper atmosphere, where the energy finally becomes visible as light.

This distinction matters because the electrons responsible for the familiar nighttime aurora are generally not simply solar-wind electrons travelling uninterrupted from the Sun into the atmosphere. Instead, the solar wind disturbs and energises Earth's magnetosphere, and electrons from within that system are accelerated down magnetic field lines toward the atmosphere.

Geomagnetic activity also affects the size and position of the auroral oval. During quieter conditions the oval remains concentrated around high magnetic latitudes. During stronger geomagnetic disturbances it can expand toward lower latitudes, allowing aurora to become visible much farther from the polar regions than usual.

This is why space-weather measurements can be useful when planning an aurora hunt. The Kp index, for example, is one measure of global geomagnetic disturbance. You can check current conditions on our Northern Lights forecast, but remember that Kp is only part of the viewing picture: your location, darkness and especially cloud cover matter too.

Why Do the Northern Lights Move and Dance?

The aurora can appear as quiet arcs, vertical rays, rippling curtains or rapidly changing structures. These shapes are controlled by where energetic electrons enter the atmosphere and by changing electric and magnetic conditions within the magnetosphere and ionosphere.

As those conditions change, different neighbouring regions of the atmosphere light up with different intensities. From the ground, the illuminated structures can appear to fold, ripple, brighten and sweep across the sky. During an active display, those changes can happen remarkably quickly.

Why Are the Northern Lights Different Colours?

The colour of an aurora depends mainly on which atmospheric gas is excited, the energy involved and the altitude of the emission. Oxygen produces the two best-known auroral colours — green and red — while nitrogen contributes blue, pink and purple tones. Overlapping emissions can create additional shades.

Green

Green is the most familiar auroral colour. It is produced by excited atomic oxygen and is commonly associated with emissions roughly 100–200km above Earth, although auroral oxygen emissions can span a broader altitude range.

Red

Red aurora is also produced by atomic oxygen, but generally higher in the atmosphere. It is often seen above green auroral structures and can become especially conspicuous during some strong geomagnetic storms.

Purple & Violet

Purple or violet tones often involve nitrogen emissions and mixtures of colours. They can appear along the lower edges of energetic auroral curtains, particularly when a display is intense.

Blue

Blue auroral light is associated with excited nitrogen. It is less commonly dominant to the human eye than green and may be easier to distinguish in strong displays or photographs.

Yellow & White

Yellowish or whitish aurora can result when different emissions overlap and our eyes perceive their combined light. White-looking aurora can also reflect the limitations of human colour vision under low-light conditions.

Pink

Pink is strongly associated with nitrogen emissions at lower auroral altitudes and can also be enhanced by overlapping colours. Bright pink or reddish-purple lower borders can appear during energetic displays.

What Do the Northern Lights Look Like to the Naked Eye?

Yes, you can see the Northern Lights with the naked eye — but they do not always look exactly like the highly saturated photographs seen online. What you see depends heavily on the strength of the display, darkness, light pollution and how well your eyes have adapted to the dark.

A weak aurora can look like a pale arch, mist or grey-green cloud. At first glance it may even be mistaken for thin cloud, although its structure and movement can give it away. As activity strengthens, green becomes easier to see and the aurora can develop clearly defined rays, curtains and rapid movement. Very strong displays can show obvious green, red, pink or purple colour without a camera.

Cameras often record auroral colour more vividly because modern sensors can collect more light than our night-adapted eyes perceive in a brief instant. That does not mean the Northern Lights are merely a photographic effect — strong aurora can be spectacular to the naked eye — but photographs and human vision do not record a dark sky in exactly the same way.

When Do the Northern Lights Occur?

The physical processes that create aurora are not limited to winter or nighttime. Auroral activity can occur throughout the year. What changes is whether the sky is dark enough for an observer on the ground to see it.

That distinction is especially important in the far north. During summer, long daylight hours and the midnight sun can make aurora impossible to see even when activity is occurring above the atmosphere. Once sufficiently dark nights return, the viewing season begins again.

Cloud cover can hide even a strong aurora completely, while a clear, dark sky can reveal activity that would otherwise be missed. For the practical month-by-month side of planning a trip, see our guide to the best time to see the Northern Lights.

Why Are They Called the Aurora Borealis?

Northern Lights and aurora borealis refer to the same phenomenon in the Northern Hemisphere. The term combines aurora, the Roman goddess of dawn, with borealis, meaning northern. The word “aurora” was used for the phenomenon by Galileo in the early 17th century.

Different northern cultures have their own names for the lights, reflecting how long people have observed them and how deeply they feature in Arctic languages and traditions.

Northern Lights Facts

Once you understand the basic science, several facts about the aurora make much more sense:

🌍

High Above Earth

Auroras typically form roughly 80–500km above Earth's surface, high in the upper atmosphere.

🧲

Auroral Ovals

Aurora tends to form in oval-shaped regions around Earth's magnetic poles rather than evenly across the polar sky.

☀️

Driven by the Sun

Changes in the solar wind and its magnetic field can transfer energy into Earth's magnetosphere and increase auroral activity.

🎨

Green Is Oxygen

The familiar green aurora comes from excited atomic oxygen; oxygen can also produce high-altitude red aurora.

🌌

Both Hemispheres

The Northern Lights are aurora borealis; the Southern Hemisphere equivalent is the aurora australis.

📷

Cameras See Differently

Camera sensors can reveal stronger colour and detail than human low-light vision, especially during weaker displays.

🪐

Earth Isn't Alone

Auroral phenomena have also been observed on other worlds, including Jupiter and Saturn.

🌙

Not Just Winter

Aurora can occur year-round, but high-latitude summer daylight often prevents it from being visible from the ground.

📡

Part of Space Weather

Strong geomagnetic storms can affect radio communications, satellites and power systems as well as producing aurora.

Where to See the Northern Lights

The Northern Lights are most reliably seen from locations beneath or close to the auroral oval. For travellers in Europe, that makes northern Norway, Swedish Lapland, Finnish Lapland and Iceland some of the most practical bases for an aurora trip.

Frequently Asked Questions

🌌 What are the Northern Lights?
The Northern Lights, or aurora borealis, are a natural light display produced when energetic charged particles — mainly electrons from Earth's magnetosphere — collide with oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere. Those gases become excited and then release energy as visible light.
⚡ What causes the Northern Lights?
The Northern Lights are driven by interactions between the solar wind and Earth's magnetosphere. Energy transferred into the magnetosphere accelerates electrons along magnetic field lines toward the polar upper atmosphere, where collisions with oxygen and nitrogen create auroral light.
🔬 How are the Northern Lights formed?
Auroras form when energy from the solar wind is transferred into Earth's magnetosphere. Electrons are accelerated toward the upper atmosphere along magnetic field lines, collide with atmospheric gases and excite them. As the gases return to lower-energy states, they release photons of light.
🧲 How do the Northern Lights work?
The Northern Lights are the visible end result of energy moving through the Sun–Earth system. The solar wind interacts with Earth's magnetic environment, magnetospheric electrons are accelerated toward the polar atmosphere, and collisions with oxygen and nitrogen convert some of that energy into visible light.
📛 Are the Northern Lights and aurora borealis the same thing?
Yes. “Northern Lights” is the everyday English name for the aurora borealis, the polar aurora of the Northern Hemisphere. In the Southern Hemisphere, the corresponding phenomenon is called the aurora australis or Southern Lights.
💚 Why are the Northern Lights green?
Green aurora is produced by excited atomic oxygen and is commonly associated with emissions roughly 100–200km above Earth. Oxygen can also produce red light at higher altitudes, while nitrogen contributes blue, pink and purple tones.
🎨 What causes different Northern Lights colours?
Aurora colour depends on the atmospheric gas being excited, the altitude and the energy of the incoming particles. Oxygen is responsible for the familiar green and red emissions, while nitrogen contributes blue, pink and purple. Overlapping emissions can create additional shades.
👁️ Can you see the Northern Lights with the naked eye?
Yes. Strong auroras can show clear green colour, structure and rapid movement to the naked eye. Weaker displays can look pale, whitish or grey-green. Cameras often record stronger colour because their sensors can collect more light than human night vision.
🌙 Do the Northern Lights happen every night?
Auroral activity is continually occurring somewhere around the polar auroral ovals, but that does not mean a visible display is guaranteed from one location every night. Darkness, cloud cover, geographic position and geomagnetic activity all influence whether you can see it.
📏 How high are the Northern Lights?
Auroras typically form in Earth's upper atmosphere at roughly 80–500km above the surface, although much of the familiar visible aurora occurs within a narrower portion of that range.
